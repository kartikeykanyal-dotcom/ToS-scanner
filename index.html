Read the red flags before you click “I agree”.

ToS-Scanner reads a Terms of Service agreement, splits it into sentences and highlights the ones that look like known unfair clauses: unilateral changes, arbitrary termination, forced arbitration, broad data sharing and more. It runs on your own machine, so the document never leaves it.

The problem

Almost nobody reads the contract they sign

Every social network, marketplace, streaming service and cloud app asks for acceptance of a Terms of Service. The click counts as informed consent, but the documents are long, dense and rewritten often. Studies cited in the report show only a very small fraction of people read them.

Internet users lack the time and legal expertise to read lengthy Terms of Service, often blindly agreeing to predatory clauses that compromise their privacy and data rights.

In scope: English agreements, sentence-level analysis, local Streamlit app. Out of scope: rewriting clauses, comparing versions, other languages, jurisdiction-specific legal analysis.

How it works

Eight stages from document to red flags

The pipeline is linear. Each stage takes the previous stage’s output, and each is a separate module that can be tested or replaced alone.

CleaningWhitespace, non-ASCII junk, quotes and dashes
SegmentationspaCy sentence splitter; fragments under about 25 characters dropped
TF-IDFUnigram and bigram vectors, up to 20,000 terms

Four layers

PresentationStreamlit interface: input widgets, highlighted results, summary and export.
ApplicationController that runs the pipeline in order, caches the model and handles errors.
NLP / MLPreprocessor, spaCy segmenter, TF-IDF vectoriser, classifier, risk scorer.
Data & modelLabelled clause corpus, serialised model (joblib) and the category taxonomy.

Because the layers are separate, the interface could become a command-line tool or browser extension, and the classifier could become a transformer, without touching the other layers.

What it looks for

Ten kinds of potentially unfair clause

The first eight follow the CLAUDETTE research corpus, which annotated the Terms of Service of about fifty platforms under European consumer law. The last two come from the project objectives and use clauses labelled by the author.

Limitation of liability

“We are not liable for any damages arising from use of the service.”

You cannot recover losses even when the provider is at fault.

Unilateral termination

“We may suspend or terminate your account at any time for any reason.”

Loss of account, content or paid benefits without recourse.

Unilateral change

“We may modify these Terms at any time without prior notice.”

The provider can alter the bargain after you agreed.

Content removal

“We may remove any content at our sole discretion.”

Your content can be deleted without explanation.

Contract by using

“By using the service you agree to these Terms.”

Consent is inferred from use, not from an explicit act.

Choice of law

“These Terms are governed by the laws of a named foreign state.”

Your consumer-protection rights may be displaced.

Jurisdiction

“All disputes shall be heard exclusively in the courts of City X.”

An inconvenient or costly forum for the consumer.

Arbitration

“Disputes shall be resolved by binding arbitration; class actions are waived.”

You give up the right to sue in court.

Data sharing / privacy

“We may share your information with our partners and affiliates.”

Personal data passes to third parties without clear limits.

Hidden fees

“Fees may apply and may be changed without notice.”

Unexpected charges. Dashed cards are not in the original CLAUDETTE label set.

The model

A small model you can explain in a paragraph

Each sentence becomes a TF-IDF vector. Rare, telling terms such as “arbitration” or “sole discretion” get high weight, and common ones such as “the” get little. Bigrams like “without notice” and “binding arbitration” are kept because the pair means more than either word.

tf-idf(t, s) = tf(t, s) × idf(t) idf(t) = ln( (1 + N) / (1 + df(t)) ) + 1

Why logistic regression

It returns real probabilities, which the adjustable threshold needs, and its weights show which terms push a sentence toward “unfair”.

P(y=1 | x) = 1 / (1 + e^-(w·x + b))

Class imbalance

Only about one sentence in ten is unfair. A model that says “fair” every time would look accurate and be useless, so training uses class weights and evaluation uses precision, recall and F1 on the unfair class.

w_c = n / (K × n_c)

Categories stay transparent

The statistical model decides whether a sentence is flagged. A dictionary of characteristic phrases then names the category, so the user can see why. A flagged sentence with no matching phrase is shown as “Other potentially unfair”.

The procedure

Input:  D  raw document     M  trained model     T  threshold
Output: F  list of flagged clauses

 1  S ← split_sentences( clean_text(D) )          # spaCy
 2  F ← [ ]
 3  for each sentence s in S:
 4      P ← M.predict_probability(s)
 5      if P > T:  append (s, category(s), P) to F
 6  render F as highlighted red flags

Runtime grows linearly with document length: segmentation is linear, TF-IDF is O(nL) and a linear model’s sparse dot product is cheap. The risk level is the share of sentences flagged: above 15% is High, above 7% is Medium, otherwise Low.

Requirements at a glance

  • Hardware. Core i5 or Ryzen 5, 8 GB RAM (16 GB recommended), 256 GB SSD. No GPU.
  • Performance. A 10,000-word document in under ten seconds once the model is loaded.
  • Privacy. Processed locally, never transmitted, not stored after the session.
  • Reliability. An empty file or an image-only PDF gives a clear message, not a crash.
  • Portability. Windows, Linux and macOS with Python 3.9+.

Safeguards in the design

Input validation

Empty text, non-PDF files and PDFs without a text layer are rejected before processing.

Size limits

Upload size and segmenter text length are capped so one huge file cannot exhaust memory.

Output escaping

Sentences are HTML-escaped, so a document containing markup cannot change the page.

No persistence

Documents stay in memory for the request and are never written to disk or logged.

Testing and evaluation

How the classifier is judged

The labelled sentences are split 70% train, 15% validation and 15% test, stratified. Duplicate boilerplate sentences are removed before splitting so identical text never sits in both train and test. The threshold T is chosen to maximise F1 on the validation set. The test set is used once, at the end. Five-fold cross-validation estimates the spread.

Metrics reported: precision, recall and F1 of the unfair class, PR-AUC, confusion matrix, recall per category, and processing time for 1,000, 5,000 and 10,000 words. Moving T up raises precision and lowers recall, so a cautious user can lower it and read a longer list.

The report leaves its result tables (model comparison, threshold sweep, per-category recall) to be filled in from the author’s own training runs. They are not invented, so this site shows no accuracy figures yet.

Limits and responsibility

A tool for pointing, not for judging

Limitations

  • One sentence at a time. Unfairness spread across sentences, or defined elsewhere in the document, can be missed.
  • Depends on training data. Agreements very unlike the corpus, such as a bank’s, may be handled less well.
  • English only, text PDFs only. Scanned documents need OCR, which is not included.
  • Keyword categories. Unusual wording can be flagged but labelled generically.

Ethical and legal notes

  • Not legal advice. The interface says so, and big decisions deserve a professional.
  • No false reassurance. No red flags does not mean safe, so the summary never says “safe”.
  • Privacy. Local processing fits the data-minimisation principle of the GDPR.
  • Bias. Labels reflect European consumer law, and other jurisdictions may judge a clause differently.

Where it could go next

  1. Transformer modelsFine-tune LEGAL-BERT and compare against the linear baseline.
  2. Context-awareUse neighbouring sentences, as memory networks do.
  3. Trained categoriesReplace phrase matching with a multi-label classifier.
  4. Plain-language reasonsOne sentence on why a clause is risky.
  5. Version comparisonHighlight what changed between two versions.
  6. Browser extensionScan on the page where terms are accepted.
  7. OCR and HindiScanned PDFs, screenshots and more languages.
  8. Feedback loopLet users mark flags right or wrong, then retrain.
